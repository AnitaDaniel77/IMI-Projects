Coding Interview Preparation

Reading: Interview Types You Might Expect

An interview aims to find a good match between company and candidate - every company runs the process differently, but most draw from a similar set of stages.

1. Screening

Usually the first contact, typically a phone call with a recruiter/HR representative - no technical questions at this stage. Purpose: gauge genuine interest and fit, and give the candidate a chance to learn about the role too.

Good questions to ask during a screening:

  • What is the role, exactly?
  • What coding language does the company typically use?
  • What does the interview process look like, and how many stages are there?
  • What's the nature of the project this role would work on?
  • What's the typical team makeup?
  • Who's on the interview panel, and what's their role?

Real-life example: a screening call is like a first date rather than a job trial - it's about whether both sides want to keep going, not about proving competence yet.

2. Quiz

An early filtering step - straightforward questions to confirm basic competency before investing in deeper interviews. Not deep or extended; concise, direct answers expected. Sample questions given:

  • How do you test for nulls in an array in a language of your choice?
  • What is the space complexity for Quicksort?
  • Which data structure would you use to store a list of keys and values?
  • What is a collision in hashing?
  • What is the syntax to select a column name using SQL?
  • What testing processes are you familiar with?

Tip: before this stage specifically, identify which languages/technologies the company actually uses (from the job posting and screening call) and focus prep there rather than studying broadly.

3. Online coding assignment

Usually happens before the technical interview (results can become a discussion point later), though sometimes after, to compare candidates. Similar difficulty to the quiz - basics like loops, booleans, conditionals. Done in a non-pressurized environment; if timed, reference material and search access may be allowed.

4. The technical interview (coding challenge)

Usually comes after screening, virtual or in-person. A problem is presented with limited time to solve it. Deliberately kept at low difficulty on the raw problem-solving side - interviewers know there's no Google access and syntax may be rusty from memory. The real focus is demonstrating problem-solving, not flawless syntax.

Talking out loud while working matters here specifically - e.g. explaining WHY one data structure was chosen over another before committing to it, not just stating the choice.

Some technical interviews are conducted purely in pseudocode. Short, specialized prompts are also common:

  • How would you explain technology X to a non-technical person?
  • What is your favorite technology and why?
  • What databases have you worked with?
  • Tell me about a technical challenge you've overcome in a project.
  • What projects have you worked on in your spare time?

Tip: prepare a few clear answers about your own programming journey ahead of time, and review your own past projects specifically for the reasoning behind technology choices you made - "why X over Y" is a genuinely common follow-up.

5. The take-home assignment

A strong sign of progress - companies don't typically ask for this unless you're already a serious candidate. Can happen before or after the technical interview, or between multiple technical rounds. Several days given to build something more substantial (an app, a routing algorithm, a domain-specific task like object detection for a vision-processing role).

Top tips from the reading

  • Research before every interview
  • Review each interview after it happens
  • Ask for feedback - most companies will give it, and it's genuinely useful prep for the next round
  • "Every interview is a learning process" - a missed question is an opportunity to learn that specific gap afterward, not just a loss
