Coding Interview Preparation

Video: More Advice from Meta Engineers

Featuring the same panel as before: Mari Batilando (FB Web3 Monetization), Moxxy Herrera (Social Impact org, Menlo Park), Julie (IG Shopping, New York), Chanel Johnson (Facebook app core architecture, remote).

The coding interview is the most stressful part

Roughly half of software developers, per employment-site survey data, find the coding interview the most stressful part of the whole process. One engineer's own prep trick: talking through a solution out loud to a stuffed animal, specifically to force the habit of narrating end-to-end as if speaking to a real interviewer.

Real-life example: talking to a stuffed animal isn't as silly as it sounds - it's the same principle as rehearsing a speech out loud instead of just reading it silently. Speaking it changes how well you actually know it.

Three interview types, with rough timing

  • Technical (LeetCode-style) - 2 to 4 of these across the process; one or two act as an initial screen, more follow after passing. Roughly 20-30 minutes per question, classic coding problems.
  • Architecture - about 45 minutes to an hour, building an end-to-end feature. Can be product-oriented (e.g. "build Tetris") or backend-oriented (data flow, scaling to many users).
  • Behavioral - questions about collaboration, challenges faced, exciting projects. One engineer's go-to question: "tell me about a time working on a team when things didn't go well" - specifically because Meta work is collaborative, not solitary, so this tests communication and growth.

Domain-specific technical questions (iOS example given)

An iOS-focused interview might ask something like "how would you build the Newsfeed surface in the Facebook app - what objects, how do they talk to each other, what networking APIs?" High-level architectural reasoning for the specific domain, not just generic algorithms.

The most common mistake flagged here: a strong domain specialist (e.g. iOS) can still fail because their general algorithms/data-structures skills are weak. These general fundamentals come up regardless of which pipeline is being interviewed for - domain skill doesn't substitute for them.

Practicing out loud, specifically

Advice repeated from an earlier video, reinforced here: practice explaining your thought process while whiteboarding with friends or colleagues - are you considering time complexity? Space? Why this approach over another? These are exactly what interviewers are listening for, because they reflect real on-the-job thinking, not just interview performance.

The interviewer's actual mindset

Explicitly stated: interviewers are rooting for candidates to succeed. Nobody wants to watch someone struggle silently for 45 minutes - the goal is genuinely collaborative problem-solving, which gives the interviewer better signal to make a good decision either way.

Getting stuck - what NOT to do

Saying nothing while stuck is described as the worst option, because the interviewer can't read minds. Explaining what you're thinking, how you're stuck, and why - even without reaching the right answer - can be enough to advance, because it demonstrates real understanding of the problem.

Real-life example: going silent when stuck is like a mechanic staring quietly at an engine with no explanation - the customer has no idea if progress is being made or if the mechanic is simply lost. Narrating "I think it's this, let me check that" builds confidence even without an instant fix.

What genuinely impresses these interviewers

  • Coming prepared with real research on the company and its values - and being able to connect that to what the candidate personally brings
  • Taking feedback well during the interview itself - if pushed a bit further on something already correct, responding well to that push is read as a strong signal for how someone would work in practice
  • Genuine enthusiasm and a positive demeanor - read as a sign of real passion for the work, which is tied directly to long-term success

Closing framing

Interviewing is described as fundamentally about showing who you are and how you think and grow - no single trick works for everyone; it's about finding how to authentically display that for yourself. On the other side, the reward is described as genuinely significant: working at scale, alongside strong engineers, with real room to grow quickly.
