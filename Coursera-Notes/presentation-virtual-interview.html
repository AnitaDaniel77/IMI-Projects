Reading: Interview Tips

Reframing the interview process

Even a strong, well-suited candidate can lose out to a more experienced one interviewing for the same role - that doesn't mean the interview itself went badly. Two useful self-check questions regardless of outcome: did I present myself to the best of my ability, and what can I learn from this for the next one?

Real-life example: this is like losing a sports match to a genuinely stronger opponent - a good performance and a loss aren't contradictory, and the real measure is whether you played your actual best, not just the scoreline.

Common standard questions worth pre-writing answers for

  • Tell me about yourself
  • Why should we hire you?
  • What are your major strengths / weaknesses?
  • What pay are you expecting?
  • How do your previous experiences make you suitable for this role?
  • What do your friends say about you?
  • Why do you want this role?
  • How have you dealt with conflict in the past?

Tip: review your own resume before an interview specifically to spot which parts connect to this particular company and role - the answers to most of these questions already live there, just not yet phrased as a story.

On weaknesses specifically: a weakness can also be a strength depending on context - "I focus excessively on details" is a liability under tight deadlines, but genuinely valuable for a role that demands precision. Frame based on what the role actually needs.

Researching the company itself

  • Where is the company based, and what do they actually do?
  • How does your past experience overlap with their current needs?
  • What's the market salary for this role (in case it comes up) - the interviewer already knows the answer, so answering well demonstrates real preparation, not just guessing

Soft skill focus: conflict resolution

Working life will inevitably involve situations where your approach doesn't align with a colleague, employer, or customer. Worth reflecting ahead of time: recall a real past conflict, how it was resolved, and honestly assess whether that approach actually worked - if not, it's worth researching better conflict-resolution approaches before the interview, not during it.

Presentation and demeanor

  • "Dress for the job you want" - appropriate, neat attire; future colleagues may literally be on the panel
  • Stay calm, don't interrupt a question - let it finish, take a breath, then answer
  • Competency questions specifically call for a STAR-shaped answer
  • Show genuine pride in past work, not false modesty
  • Come with your own questions ready - interviewers should give you the chance to ask; use it to learn whether the company's goals genuinely align with yours
  • Be authentic rather than exaggerating - exaggeration might get a follow-up interview, but being genuinely capable in the role matters more long-term

Virtual interview specifics

  • Body language reads differently over video - harder to interpret than in person
  • Avoid talking over the interviewer, since audio delay/quality makes this worse on video than in person
  • Test everything beforehand: computer charged, working earphones as backup, camera permissions granted, connection speed checked
  • Dress the same as you would in person, even from home
  • Sit up straight, stay attentive - home environment doesn't lower the expected decorum
  • Choose a quiet, distraction-free location - avoid interruptions from others in the space

Real-life example: preparing tech for a virtual interview is like a musician checking their instrument is tuned before walking on stage - it's not the performance itself, but skipping it risks undermining a performance that's otherwise ready.

Closing framing

Same theme as the earlier readings: an interview is a learning experience regardless of outcome, and being prepared and ready is what actually determines success over time, not any single interview's result.
