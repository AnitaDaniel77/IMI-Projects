Reading: Recap - Accessibility

What web accessibility actually covers

Making websites usable for people with disabilities, impairments, or limits - blindness, poor vision, learning difficulties, cognitive impairments, deafness, hearing loss, speech impairments. This isn't optional in most developed countries - it's a legal requirement, not just good practice.

Real-life example: accessibility is like a building having both stairs and a ramp - the ramp isn't a bonus feature, it's what makes the building actually usable for everyone who needs to get in.

The four WCAG principles (POUR)

Perceivable

Content must be perceivable somehow - not necessarily seen with the eyes. Screen readers convert text to speech or braille for blind/low-vision users.

<img src={heroImage} alt="Little Lemon signature dish" />
{/* alt text is what makes an image PERCEIVABLE to someone
    using a screen reader - without it, the image is just
    invisible to that user, not just unseen */}

Real-life example: alt text is like a museum audio guide describing a painting for a visitor who can't see it - the content still reaches them, just through a different channel.

Operable

Every feature must be usable by all - navigation, menu links, play/pause controls. Simple, uncluttered sites tend to be the most operable, since unnecessary complexity is what tends to block users with disabilities.

<button onClick={handleSubmit}>Submit reservation</button>
{/* a real <button> is keyboard-operable by default (Tab
    to focus, Enter/Space to activate) - a <div onClick={...}>
    styled to look like a button is NOT keyboard-operable
    unless extra work is done to make it so */}

Real-life example: operability is like a door that opens whether you push it, use a wheelchair-accessible button, or ask someone to hold it - multiple ways to achieve the same action, none of them blocked.

Understandable

Content and navigation should be clear and logically organized. Overly complex language restricts access for people with cognitive differences and non-native speakers alike. Navigation should stay consistent and accessible across pages.

Real-life example: understandable design is like airport signage using simple icons and short words instead of dense paragraphs - clarity benefits literally everyone passing through, not just people who "need" the simpler version.

Robust

Content should work reliably across assistive technologies - a screen reader (or any other tool) should be able to parse and present it correctly, not just a typical browser.

<nav>{/* semantic nav landmark - screen readers can jump
         straight to "navigation" as a recognized region */}
  <ul>
    <li><Link to="/">Home</Link></li>
  </ul>
</nav>

Real-life example: robustness is like a document that opens correctly whether it's read on a phone, a laptop, or converted to audio - the structure holds up regardless of what's reading it.

Pseudocode for an accessibility self-check

FOR each image on the page:
    does it have meaningful alt text?

FOR each interactive element (buttons, links, form fields):
    can it be reached and activated using ONLY a keyboard?
    does it have a visible focus state?

FOR each form field:
    is it properly linked to its label (htmlFor / id)?

FOR the page overall:
    is heading structure logical (h1 -> h2 -> h3, not skipped)?
    does color alone convey any required information?

Tip for applying this practically: the fastest real test isn't reading a checklist, it's literally unplugging your mouse and trying to use the whole booking flow with only Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, and arrow keys. Anywhere you get stuck or lost is exactly where a keyboard-only or screen-reader user would get stuck too.
