Coding Interview Preparation

Verbal and Non-Verbal Communication

Interview success depends heavily on communication - conveying suitability clearly, both through what's said and how it's presented.

Non-verbal communication

  • Punctuality - arrive around 10 minutes early, especially if the venue is unfamiliar; arriving flustered undercuts composure
  • Eye contact and active listening throughout
  • Appropriate dress - clean, neat, professional attire generally expected
  • Posture - avoid squirming, face-touching, or hand-wringing; nervous gestures can unintentionally read as unpreparedness

Tip: doing genuine research on the role and company beforehand is framed as the real cure for nerves - not just a box to check, but the actual mechanism that settles anxiety.

Verbal communication

The 80/20 rule: a good interviewer speaks roughly 20% of the time, letting the candidate speak the other 80%. Practical implications:

  • Let the interviewer finish the full question before answering
  • Keep answers clear and concise - resist the urge to dump everything you know on a topic, which leads to rambling; a focused answer leaves room for natural follow-up questions
  • Avoid self-deprecating or negative framing
Instead of: "I failed at that task."
Try: "That task was challenging, and it gave me ideas for
      areas I want to explore further."

Real-life example: reframing a setback this way is like a chef describing a dish that didn't work as "a good test of a new technique" rather than "a disaster" - same event, but one framing shows growth and the other just shows discouragement.

Also avoid excessive slang, cursing, or inappropriate humor.

The STAR method

A structured way to answer behavioral/technical questions with real depth:

  • Situation - what was the context? What project, what challenge?
  • Task - what were your specific responsibilities in that situation?
  • Action - what did you actually do to address it?
  • Result - what was the outcome, and how did your approach shape that result?

Real-life example: STAR is like a well-told story with a clear arc - setting, conflict, what the character does, and how it resolves. A rambling answer with no structure is like a story with no ending - the listener isn't sure what actually happened or why it mattered.

Applying STAR to a real example

A genuinely usable one from recent project work: debugging the React Router version mismatch and the fetchAPI global-scope issue in the Little Lemon capstone.

  • Situation - the booking page went completely blank after connecting the app to a real external API
  • Task - needed to identify why, since the error messages pointed in a few different directions across several attempts
  • Action - systematically isolated each layer: confirmed the script was loading (Network tab), checked whether the function existed globally (Console), and eventually traced it to a JavaScript scoping quirk (const vs var at global script scope) rather than a code logic bug
  • Result - fixed the actual root cause instead of just patching the symptom, and the fix held up cleanly through the rest of the project's testing

Tip: having 2-3 STAR-shaped stories ready ahead of time - not memorized word for word, just the shape of situation/task/action/result - makes it much easier to answer "tell me about a challenge you overcame" without freezing up in the moment.

Recap

Interviewers are watching for candidates who can clearly convey a concept - both verbally and non-verbally. STAR is presented as the reliable framework specifically for the questions that come up repeatedly across almost any interview.
