Lesson: UX, Form Validation, and Accessibility

What UX actually means here

Every user's experience is unique, and the person who designed a product is often not one of its actual users - which means assumptions about what users want are risky. The way to actually find out is to get close to real users: interact with them, observe how they use the product, and ask why they made certain choices.

Real-life example: this is like a restaurant owner assuming their own tastes represent every customer, when the only way to actually know what customers want is to watch what they order and ask why - not guess from behind the kitchen door.

Form validation is emotional, not just technical

Form validation technically means checking whether submitted data is accurate - but the lesson stresses this is more emotional than technical. A form needs to clearly alert users when something's wrong, or reassure them when it's right - forms are already tiresome to fill out, so the goal is making that experience as painless as possible.

Real-life example: a validation error is like a helpful shop assistant catching a mistake before you leave the till - "your card didn't go through" said kindly and clearly, versus silence that leaves you wondering if anything worked at all.

Accessibility - designing for everyone

Beyond legal compliance, the actual goal is usability for everyone regardless of how they encounter the product - different abilities, different assistive technologies, different contexts.

Heuristic evaluation

A heuristic evaluation compares an interface against established, accepted usability principles - not against personal opinion, but against a known framework (referencing the earlier UX/UI principles course) to judge how usable the Little Lemon reservation interface actually is.

Real-life example: a heuristic evaluation is like a building inspector checking a house against a known safety code, rather than just walking through and saying whether they personally like it.
