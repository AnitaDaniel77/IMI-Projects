Software Engineering Code of Ethics

Where it comes from

Developed by a Joint Task Force formed by IEEE-CS (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Computer Society) and ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) — created because of how prominent computing has become in commerce, government, and society. Its purpose is holding software engineers accountable, to protect the field's status as a beneficial, respected profession. It applies to anyone in or connected to the profession: engineers, mentors, instructors, managers, students, policymakers.

Covers the full software lifecycle — specification, analysis, design, development, testing, maintenance — and is fundamentally dedicated to serving the public interest.

The eight principles

1. Public

Comes first deliberately — software engineers should act primarily to positively affect the public good. Includes accepting responsibility for work regarding safety, fairness, accessibility, and integrity.

2. Client / Employer

Act in the best interests of clients and employers. Be honest and forthright about unethical actions (plagiarism, illegal activity), seek consent where appropriate, and honor confidentiality.

3. Product

Seek quality in the product, while realistically balancing cost and timelines.

4. Judgment

Act with integrity and independence in professional judgment — maintain objectivity and honesty with the software and documentation involved. Must not engage in inappropriate financial activity (bribery, double billing) or accept duties creating a conflict of interest.

5. Management

Engineering managers and leaders should uphold these same principles toward the people they manage: minimize risk, employ security procedures, set realistic expectations for staff, provide fair compensation, and secure intellectual property rights where appropriate.

6. Profession

Protect the reputation of the profession — act with integrity, don't elevate yourself at others' expense. Communicate intent to comply with this code to managers/employers/clients, and raise concern when the code is being violated.

7. Colleagues

Treat colleagues with respect and fairness — encourage peers to comply with the code, and never take credit for someone else's work.

8. Self

Commit to lifelong learning and professional development — strive to build quality software and conduct yourself professionally.

Real banking example

Several of these principles have direct, high-stakes weight in banking specifically: the Public principle (safety, fairness, accessibility, integrity) applies directly to something like a fraud-detection feature — getting it wrong could unfairly flag or fail to protect real people. The Judgment principle's ban on conflicts of interest is core to financial services compliance generally. And Colleagues/Profession map directly onto the peer-review, mentoring, and pair-programming culture you're already living through Ngoako's feedback and Hilbert's SME sessions — not taking credit for a fix someone else guided you to, and encouraging your own peers toward good practice.

An important caveat

This code isn't a substitute for conscientious decision-making and common sense — it's a supplemental guide. Knowing when and how to actually apply these principles in a specific situation still comes down to individual discretion and judgment.

Logic to hold onto

Notice the code's structure moves outward from the broadest responsibility to the most personal one: Public (society) → Client/Employer (the people paying for the work) → Product (the actual thing being built) → Judgment (your own professional integrity) → Management (those you lead) → Profession (the field as a whole) → Colleagues (peers) → Self (your own growth). It's a widening-then-narrowing circle of who your ethical obligations extend to — starting and ending with responsibility, just at different scales.
