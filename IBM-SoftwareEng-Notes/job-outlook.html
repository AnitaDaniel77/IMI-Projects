Job Outlook for Software Engineers

Why demand is high

Almost every industry needs software to compete and grow — most organizations depend on applications and websites, whether internal-facing or customer-facing. The Internet of Things is driving continued demand for software that interacts with physical products. Competitive pressure between vendors also fuels demand — every time one vendor updates their product, competitors need to update theirs too, requiring more engineers to build and maintain those releases.

The numbers

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics predicted 22% job growth for software developers, analysts, and testers from 2020-2030 — well above average — meaning roughly 200,000 new openings per year in the US alone. Growth spans many industries, from mobile app development to health and insurance. Security software specifically is expected to grow significantly, driven by rising cybersecurity threats and cybercrime.

Who gets hired

Many software engineers hold degrees in software engineering or computer science, but companies like IBM, Google, and Tesla increasingly hire non-graduates who can demonstrate the required skills directly. This widens access to the field, so the cost of a formal degree doesn't automatically exclude capable candidates.

Compensation

Salaries scale meaningfully with experience — in the US, roughly $90,000 for a junior role up to $120,000+ for an experienced one, averaging around $110,000, more than 2.5x the US average base salary. Compensation is often paired with benefits like medical insurance, gym membership, profit shares, and retirement plans.

What to expect day-to-day

  • Dress codes tend to be informal, unless in a customer-facing role.
  • Hours are often flexible, sometimes centered on core hours in the middle of the day.
  • Occasional meetings or calls may require presence at a specific time.
  • Hours tend to increase near release dates — often without a corresponding pay increase.
  • Remote work flexibility varies by employer, though nothing technologically prevents it — some teams are globally distributed, working across all hours.

Who employs software engineers

Virtually any kind of business — from FAANG-scale tech companies (Facebook, Amazon, Apple, Netflix, Google) to mid-sized specialized software companies, small startups, and non-technology companies (banks, retailers, pharmaceuticals) needing engineers for their own internal or external systems. Most employers also encourage continual learning — investing in keeping technical and soft skills current.

Real banking example

Capitec falling into the "large non-technology company needing internal software engineers" category is worth sitting with — banking software careers aren't a lesser or side-path version of "real" tech work, they're one of the explicitly named categories this lesson lists as a major employer of software engineers.

Employment options beyond full-time

  • Employed roles — apprenticeship, internship, part-time, or full-time; offers stability and regular income.
  • Independent contracting / gig work — a notably large market in software specifically, ranging from simple website builds to highly specialized skills, offered on a time-based, project-based, or freelance basis.
  • Open source volunteering — coding for free on open source projects; builds real experience, sharpens skills, improves future employment prospects, and raises your technical visibility/reputation — alongside the personal satisfaction of contributing to something you care about.

Logic to hold onto

The through-line across this whole lesson: software engineering is genuinely one of the more flexible careers available, both in how you enter it (degree or demonstrated skill) and how you practice it (employed, contract, freelance, or volunteer). That flexibility is worth remembering specifically because your own path — trainee programme plus self-directed courses — is exactly the kind of entry route this lesson describes as increasingly normalized, not a workaround.
