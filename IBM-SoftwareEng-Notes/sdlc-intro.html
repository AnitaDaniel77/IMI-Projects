Introduction to the SDLC

What it is

The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a systematic process for building high-quality software within a predictable timeframe and budget, aimed at meeting the client's actual business requirements. It breaks development into distinct phases, each with its own process and deliverables, and it can be run as a cycle — planning, design, development, repeat — rather than a strict one-shot process.

Real banking example

Think of a Capitec project like building a new "apply for a credit card" feature in the app. Without an SDLC, a team might jump straight into coding a form and hope it works. With the SDLC, the team first plans (what does compliance require on this form, what does risk need to check), designs the flow, builds it, tests it against real scenarios, deploys it, and then maintains it as regulations or fraud patterns change. Each phase has a clear owner and clear output before the next phase starts.

Where it came from

The SDLC took shape in the mid-1960s as software started getting genuinely complex — large corporations needed to manage big business systems with heavy computational needs, and ad hoc coding wasn't cutting it anymore. The original approach was the waterfall method: strictly linear, one phase fully finishes before the next starts. Over time, the industry moved toward more iterative methods that could adapt to shifting customer requirements instead of locking everything in upfront (waterfall vs. iterative approaches get covered properly in a later lesson).

Why businesses use it — key advantages

  • A defined process instead of ad hoc work — improves efficiency, reduces risk.
  • Clear discrete phases — team members know exactly what they're working on and when, and cross-domain teams know when their part is done and it's safe to hand off to the next phase.
  • Better communication — stakeholders can see where they fit into the overall process, not just their own slice of it.
  • Room for iteration — a cycle can loop back to absorb new or changed requirements instead of treating the first plan as final.
  • Early problem-solving — issues get caught in the design phase rather than discovered mid-coding, which is far cheaper to fix early.
  • Well-defined roles — reduces conflict and overlapping responsibilities across the team.

Real banking example

The "catch problems early" advantage is exactly why Capitec's IMI programme has you doing capstones and structured reviews at each stage rather than just handing you a huge project and checking in at the very end — the SDLC principle of "fix it in design, not in code" is the same reasoning behind breaking your own trainee programme into weekly milestones instead of one giant deliverable in January.

Logic to hold onto

The SDLC isn't really about the specific phases (those come in a later lesson) — it's about the underlying principle: structure reduces risk. Every advantage listed here traces back to one idea — knowing what phase you're in, what's expected, and who owns it prevents the chaos, cost overruns, and buggy releases that defined the pre-SDLC "software crisis" from the last lesson.
