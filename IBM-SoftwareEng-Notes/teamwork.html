Teamwork and Squads

What makes teamwork actually work

A team is a group working toward a common aim, each person bringing different skills. Working alongside others outside your usual strengths is how you actually expand your skillset — not just working solo on what you're already good at. Collaboration also promotes creativity by giving space to challenge and refine each other's thinking.

Ingredients for good teamwork:

  • Trust and respect, built over time through everyone contributing equitably
  • Clearly defined and agreed-upon goals, so everyone knows what they're working toward
  • Clearly defined roles, to avoid duplicated effort or dropped tasks
  • Playing to each member's strengths
  • Celebrating success and honestly analyzing problems
  • Communication through a method that genuinely works for the whole team

What teamwork looks like in practice

  • Kick-off meetings — planning the project, assigning tasks, agreeing on goals at the start.
  • Regular team/sub-team meetings — reviewing progress and plans throughout the project.
  • Design and code reviews — requested at team level, done by whoever's available.
  • Walkthroughs — team members present their section of work to the rest of the team for shared oversight; key members also present walkthroughs to stakeholders.
  • Retrospectives — held after a project completes, reviewing what went well and what to improve.
  • Mentoring — may or may not involve someone on your current project team; sometimes mutual, so everyone learns from everyone.
  • Internal projects — some teams handle cross-cutting work like defining coding standards, maintaining legacy code, or evaluating new tools for the team.

Why teamwork improves the actual code

Working in a team increases accountability — engineers are more likely to follow coding standards and document their work when others will read it. That accountability directly translates to better quality code, fewer bugs, and more maintainable code. It also reduces stress (there's always someone to ask for help) and improves problem-solving (talking through an issue with someone often surfaces the answer faster than working alone). Each member also gets a better sense of the bigger picture, leading to a more coherent overall solution than isolated individual work would produce.

Squads (Agile terminology)

In Agile organizations, a team is often called a squad — typically up to 10 developers. A squad usually includes:

  • A squad leader — acts as anchor developer and coach
  • A few software engineers — build features and write test cases
  • One or two UX designers/developers

Some squads use pair programming, where developers work in pairs (covered in more depth in a later lesson).

Logic to hold onto

Every teamwork mechanism in this lesson — kick-offs, reviews, walkthroughs, retrospectives — exists to solve the same underlying problem: keeping everyone's mental model of the project aligned as it evolves. Without those checkpoints, individual understanding drifts apart quietly until it surfaces as a bug, a missed requirement, or duplicated work. The meetings aren't overhead — they're what keeps a group of individuals actually functioning as one team.
